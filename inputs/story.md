# The Red Coat

Jack was fifteen the winter the river froze all the way across. He was a lean boy, all
elbows and knees, with a mop of dark curls he never combed and a gap between his front
teeth that showed when he grinned, which was often. His mother had made him a coat that
autumn — bright red wool, three brass buttons, a collar he could turn up against the
wind — and he wore it everywhere, so that people in the village said you could see Jack
coming from the far side of the valley.

The river was called the Marn, and it curled around the village of Holloway like an arm.
Old folk said it had never frozen solid in living memory. But that December the cold came
down out of the north and stayed, and one grey morning the Marn lay still and white and
hard as a road.

Jack was the first to walk out onto it. He went slowly at first, listening to the ice
talk beneath his boots — long groans and sudden cracks that ran away into the dark. When
it held, he laughed, and his breath hung in the air, and the red coat was the only colour
in the whole frozen world.

He was halfway across when he heard the dog. It was a small brown terrier, out on the ice
downstream, near where the current ran fastest under the surface. It had gone too far. The
ice there was thin and blue-black, and the little dog stood trembling on it, afraid to move.

Jack did not think for very long. He lay down flat on his belly — his mother had told him
once that this was what you did — and spread his arms and legs wide, and began to slide
himself toward the dog an inch at a time. The red coat scraped over the ice. Beneath him
the surface ticked and shifted.

"Easy," he said to the dog, though his own voice shook. "Easy now. I'm coming."

The terrier watched him with wet black eyes. When Jack was close enough he pulled off the
red coat, quick, and threw one end of it across the thin ice like a rope. "Bite it," he
whispered. "Go on." And the dog, as if it understood, took the wool in its teeth, and
Jack pulled — gently, gently — and dragged the little animal back across the blue-black
ice to where the white ice was thick and safe.

They lay there together a moment, boy and dog, both of them shaking. Then the ice gave a
sound like a rifle shot, and a black crack opened where the dog had been standing, and the
fast dark water showed through.

Jack got up. He put the red coat back on, and tucked the shivering terrier inside it
against his chest, and walked back to the shore very carefully, testing every step.

By the time he reached the village the whole of Holloway had come out to watch, drawn by
the red coat moving across the white river. His mother pushed through the crowd and took
his cold face in her hands and could not decide whether to scold him or weep, so she did
both. The dog put its nose out from inside the coat and licked her wrist.

They never found out whose dog it was. It stayed with Jack after that, and followed the
red coat everywhere, and the two of them grew up together in the village by the river that
froze, the winter Jack was fifteen.
